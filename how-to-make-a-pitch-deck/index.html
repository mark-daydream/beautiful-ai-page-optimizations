Start with a template and an outline

Two decisions come before the first slide. Where you will build the deck, and what it will say.

Pick the tool and a template first. Investors will read the deck in whatever format you send, so the tool mostly decides how much formatting work lands on you. Starting from a pitch deck template gives you the slide order below already laid out. The work becomes filling it in.

Working this way keeps you from polishing a slide that later gets cut. It also gives you something concrete to test on a co-founder or advisor while it still looks rough. How do I make my pitch deck look professional if I'm not a designer?

Four design rules separate professional decks from homemade ones: keep one idea per slide, use the same one or two fonts and a small color palette throughout, align every element to a consistent grid, and leave white space around your content. Investors see hundreds of decks, and these are the signals they read in the first few seconds.

Your tool determines how hard those rules are to follow. PowerPoint and Google Slides start you from a blank canvas or a template you maintain by hand, so alignment and spacing slip as slides multiply. Canva offers strong pitch deck templates, though every edit is still freeform. Beautiful.ai enforces the rules for you. Smart Slides keep layouts aligned and evenly spaced as you add content, and our pitch deck templates, including recreations of famous decks from Uber and Airbnb, give you a proven structure to start from.

Why: Verbatim. This is the strongest section on the page and the one the new FAQ block is written around, so nothing below repeats it.

New New section: build, check, and share
New

Build, check, and share the deck

With the story written and the design rules set, three steps get the deck out the door.

  1. Build it in the template. Drop each slide’s point into the matching layout and swap in your own numbers, screenshots, and logos. Set your fonts, colors, and logo once in a Theme so every slide picks them up. Fixing them slide by slide at the end takes longer.
  2. Check it with someone outside the company. Send the draft to an advisor or a founder who has raised before and ask two questions. Which slide did they stop on, and what did they still not understand at the end? Then present it aloud, timed, before the first real meeting.
  3. Export a version investors can open anywhere. Investors often read the deck before they hear it, so send a PDF export that keeps the formatting fixed on any device, and keep the editable deck for the live presentation. Beautiful.ai exports to PDF and to an editable PowerPoint file, so you can deliver whichever format the investor asks for.

A pitch deck asks someone to buy into an idea, usually an investor buying into the company. A sales deck asks a prospect to buy a solution. The two overlap on the problem and the product. Patterns & Gaps

Every guide has the slide list. The ranking ones wrap it in a process.

The slide list is table stakes

Medium, J.P. Morgan, Antler, and Startup Grind each walk through 10 to 13 slides in nearly the same order. This page has 10, written more tightly than any of them. That part stays word for word.

The process around it is missing

The Medium guide opens on choosing a tool and a template and drafting without design. J.P. Morgan and Antler close on feedback and rehearsal. Medium also ends on a shareable PDF. This page has none of the four. Two sections cover them in 360 words.

The phrase is absent

The title and H1 say “write.” The page’s largest queries say “make” and “create” with “for investors” attached, and “investors” appears in the title only inside a parenthetical. The exact primary phrase appears nowhere on the page.

Depth is a coverage gap

The four guides run 3,500 to 5,600 words against 790 here. The draft adds about 560 words in three sections and 12 links onto existing wording. Nothing in the ten slide sections is lengthened.

Name matches nothing

Host drops the www, no canonical
